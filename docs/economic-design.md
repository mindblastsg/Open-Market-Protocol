# Participation and incentive design

## Status

This is a design hypothesis for testing, not a claim about proven consumer demand or realised economic returns. Alpha transactions use fictional services and simulated money.

## The offer

Open Market Protocol should make a simple promise that can be demonstrated rather than advertised:

> See the real deal, keep the evidence, and choose across independent markets without handing one platform permanent control.

For a consumer, that should eventually mean:

- one discovery experience across participating marketplaces;
- the displayed price, fee rules, and booking terms captured at commitment time;
- a complete, portable transaction record;
- more supplier choice without creating an account for every marketplace;
- visible redress and cancellation states;
- a disclosed share of network surplus when a governed market chooses to return one.

The alpha proves only price capture and transaction history. Cross-market choice, portability, and surplus returns remain later milestones.

## Why each participant joins

### Consumers

Consumers gain broader choice, inspectable terms, and less dependence on one gatekeeper. Their strongest reason to return should be reliable utility, not points that become worthless outside one app.

### Suppliers

Suppliers gain access to demand from other compatible nodes while keeping a direct relationship with their local operator. Fees and ranking rules should be explicit, and historical rules should remain auditable.

### Marketplace operators

Operators gain a larger effective catalogue and demand pool without surrendering their brand, membership, local policy, or database. Interoperability becomes a growth channel rather than an acquisition exit.

### Protocol contributors

Contributors gain a neutral specification, conformance suite, and reference implementation that can support multiple sectors. The protocol must not make the reference implementation a privileged intermediary.

## Game-theoretic constraints

### Make cooperation individually rational

A node should federate when the incremental value of outside supply and demand exceeds integration, moderation, and dispute costs. Protocol fees must not recreate the margin burden the network is intended to reduce.

### Prevent free-riding

Nodes that consume discovery or transaction services should contribute through reciprocal routing, transparent cost recovery, or governed membership obligations. The protocol should measure useful participation without requiring one central owner.

### Avoid winner-take-all feedback

Global ranking can cause one operator or supplier to capture the entire network. Discovery should support local relevance, plural ranking policies, diversity constraints, and user-selected sorting. No founding node should receive permanent priority.

### Keep defection possible

Exportable records and replaceable operators make exit credible. Credible exit disciplines fees and governance more effectively than promises alone.

### Preserve repeated-game trust

Signed events, delivery receipts, deterministic state transitions, and append-only evidence make honest repeated interaction easier. Misbehaviour should be attributable to a node, while sanctions remain proportionate and reversible where possible.

### Do not buy fake liquidity

Subsidies can temporarily manufacture both sides of a marketplace and disappear when funding stops. Early pilots should measure repeat transactions, supplier retention, fulfilled demand, dispute rates, and cost per completed transaction without permanent incentives.

## Network bootstrapping

Start with a dense, bounded market where participants have repeated needs and existing trust. A useful launch cell has:

1. a narrow service category;
2. one accountable local operator;
3. enough suppliers to provide real choice;
4. a repeat consumer use case;
5. a neighbouring node with complementary supply;
6. a measurable cross-node transaction that cannot be served as well locally.

The first compelling federation demonstration is not “millions of listings.” It is a consumer finding a materially better option on another independent node, completing the transaction, and understanding the terms and economic trail.

## Consumer value metrics

Measure benefits against a baseline rather than relying on engagement:

- median total price and disclosed fee rate;
- successful search-to-booking rate;
- time to a suitable option;
- number of meaningfully distinct suppliers considered;
- repeat usage without promotional subsidy;
- cancellation and dispute resolution time;
- percentage of records exportable in a documented format;
- simulated or real governed surplus returned to participants;
- concentration of transactions by node and supplier.

High activity with rising concentration, hidden fees, or subsidy dependence is not success.

## Safeguards

- Never describe simulated allocations as dividends or legal entitlements.
- Separate protocol governance from the commercial interests of any one operator.
- Publish fee and ranking rule versions used for each transaction.
- Make conflicts of interest and related-party services visible.
- Protect participant privacy when publishing aggregate metrics.
- Use regulated payment providers and appropriate legal structures before handling real money.
- Test manipulation, collusion, sybil behaviour, exclusion, and discriminatory ranking before a production pilot.

## Falsifiable adoption test

The proposition is supported only if an independent operator is willing to run a node, suppliers remain active without permanent subsidy, consumers complete repeat transactions, and at least one cross-node transaction produces measurable value over the local alternative.

If that does not happen, the project should revise the incentive design rather than attributing failure to users.
